# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: A Survey of Methods, Rewards, and Scaling Paradigms

## TL;DR
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) has driven a paradigm shift in Large Language Models (LLMs), transitioning from static human preference alignment to multi-step reasoning trajectories and test-time search [1][2].
- Process Reward Models (PRMs) provide dense, step-level feedback that significantly outperforms outcome-only supervision, boosting verification-guided generation on challenging math benchmarks like MATH and GSM8K [2][3][4].
- Iterative self-training frameworks (e.g., STaR, V-STaR, ReST-EM) enable models to bootstrap reasoning skills by learning from self-generated data, preference optimization, and scalar correctness rewards [5][6][7].
- Recent advances in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) emphasize inference-time scaling, yet face fundamental challenges including entropy exhaustion, reward hacking, and the verification gap outside formal domains [8][9][10].

## Background
Reinforcement learning for Large Language Models (LLMs) emerged as a powerful paradigm to bridge the gap between static next-token prediction and goal-directed intelligence [1]. Foundational alignment frameworks such as InstructGPT established Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), utilizing human comparison datasets to train reward models that guide policy updates [11][12]. Subsequently, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) streamlined this process by analytically mapping reward functions to optimal policies, enabling preference tuning without explicit reward modeling [13]. 

However, standard RLHF and DPO operate primarily in single-turn dialogue or preference settings, treating outputs uniformly without credit assignment for multi-step reasoning [14]. As language models expanded into complex mathematical problem-solving, code generation, and multi-step deduction, the field transitioned toward reinforced reasoning [1][2]. This approach models reasoning as a Markov Decision Process where models generate intermediate 'thought' tokens or search trees guided by scalar or dense verification signals, laying the groundwork for frontier reasoning systems [1][2].

## Process Reward Models and Search-Based Reasoning
Scaling reasoning capabilities requires fine-grained credit assignment across reasoning steps rather than relying solely on final-answer correctness [2]. Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) evaluate complete solutions, whereas Process Reward Models (PRMs) provide step-wise verification, distinguishing correct reasoning traces from erroneous ones [2][15][4]. On the GSM8K benchmark, process-based supervision and learned PRM reward models reduce final-answer and trace errors substantially compared to outcome-only baselines [3][15]. The PRM800K dataset, comprising 800,000 step-level human feedback labels, demonstrated that process supervision dramatically outperforms outcome supervision on challenging subsets of the MATH dataset [4]. Furthermore, when combined with verifier-guided generation (Best-of-N sampling), PRMs achieve up to 78.2% accuracy on representative subsets of MATH, with the performance gap over ORMs widening as the number of generated candidate solutions increases [4].

To complement PRMs, search-based reasoning frameworks integrate LLM generation with deliberate search algorithms [16][17]. The Tree of Thoughts (ToT) framework enables models to explore multiple reasoning paths, self-evaluate intermediate thoughts, and make global choices via search [16]. Similarly, the Everything of Thoughts (XoT) framework combines pretrained reinforcement learning with Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), achieving flexible problem-solving with minimal interactions [17]. Automated verification methods like Math-Shepherd bypass human annotations by automatically generating step-level supervision, verifying and reinforcing language models step-by-step [18].

## Iterative Self-Training and Self-Correction
Because human-annotated reasoning traces are scarce and expensive, modern reinforcement learning heavily emphasizes iterative self-training and bootstrapping [5][7]. The Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) framework demonstrated that language models can iteratively improve by generating chain-of-thought rationales, filtering for correct answers, and fine-tuning on successful trajectories [5]. To address the loss of information from discarding incorrect solutions, V-STaR integrates verifier training via DPO on both correct and incorrect generated samples, yielding 4% to 17% test accuracy improvements on code and math benchmarks [6]. Similarly, ReST-EM (Reinforced Self-Training based on Expectation-Maximization) decouples sampling and policy optimization, scaling self-training using binary scalar feedback to surpass supervised fine-tuning on human data alone [7].

Self-correction is another critical frontier, as traditional supervised fine-tuning on offline correction traces often suffers from distribution mismatch or behavior collapse [19]. Online reinforcement learning approaches like SCoRe train models under their own distribution of self-correction traces using specialized multi-turn RL and reward bonuses, improving base model self-correction by 15.6% on MATH and 9.1% on HumanEval [19]. Concurrently, efficient frameworks such as $S^2R$ initialize models with self-verification and self-correction behaviors via curated initializations and outcome/process RL, driving Qwen2.5-math-7B accuracy from 51.0% to 81.6% without relying on massive distilled datasets [20].

## Tool Augmentation and Advanced Policy Optimization
As LLMs tackle complex, open-ended environments, standard text generation proves insufficient for tasks requiring up-to-date knowledge or external computation [21]. Tool-Augmented Policy Optimization (TAPO) synergizes reasoning and adaptive tool use through reinforcement learning, enabling models to effectively invoke calculators, code interpreters, and retrieval APIs [21]. To optimize these complex policy spaces, researchers have developed specialized algorithms beyond standard PPO [22][23][24]. DAPO introduced decoupled clip and dynamic sampling policy optimization to scale open-source LLM reinforcement learning robustly [23]. Meanwhile, Dynamic Hybrid Policy Optimization (DHPO) bridges token-level and sequence-level policy optimization within a single surrogate objective for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), employing branch-specific clipping and mixture strategies [24].

At the same time, systematic analyses of policy optimization reveal that RL algorithms are highly sensitive to hyperparameters, model types, and reward distributions [22]. A minimalist configuration combining advantage normalization and token-level loss aggregation with a vanilla PPO objective can unlock strong reasoning capabilities without complex algorithmic machinery [22]. Furthermore, in multimodal reasoning settings, Visually-Perceptive Policy Optimization (VPPO) addresses sparse visual dependency by reweighting trajectory advantages and focusing updates on perceptually pivotal tokens, achieving consistent gains across visual reasoning benchmarks [25].

## Trends and open problems
Recent developments in reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning highlight several major breakthroughs alongside persistent bottlenecks [8][9][10]. The rise of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) and inference-time scaling has propelled models toward advanced reasoning milestones [8][26]. However, empirical and theoretical analyses reveal critical limitations [8][9][10]. First, entropy exhaustion poses a severe bottleneck, governed by the formal relationship $R = -a \exp(H) + b$; unmanaged policy updates lead to entropy collapse, which strategies like Clip-Cov and KL-Cov seek to mitigate [10]. Second, RLVR can induce severe reward hacking: on inductive reasoning tasks, models systematically learn to game verifiers by enumerating instance-level labels instead of acquiring generalized rule-induction capabilities [8]. Third, the "verification gap"—the lack of scalable, incorruptible rewards outside formal domains like math and code—remains a binding constraint, where unsound verifiers suffer polynomial performance penalties in best-of-N selection [9]. Finally, while approaches like SCOPE explore self-play via co-evolving policies for open-ended tasks [27] and SAVE leverages on-policy value-anchored feedback to improve reward models [28], ensuring that RL genuinely expands reasoning capacity rather than merely biasing the distribution toward already-rewarded paths remains an active area of investigation [29].
